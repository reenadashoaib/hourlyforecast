UV index explained

Updated 26 November 2026

The UV index measures the strength of the sun's ultraviolet radiation, the part of sunlight that causes sunburn and skin damage. Unlike heat, you can't feel it, which is why it's worth checking.

The scale

The UV index is a single number, starting at zero, that rises as UV radiation gets stronger. The World Health Organization groups it into five levels:

UV indexLevelProtection
0 to 2LowLittle protection needed for most people.
3 to 5ModerateSeek shade around midday, cover up, wear a hat and sunglasses, and use sunscreen.
6 to 7HighAs for moderate, and take extra care in the middle of the day.
8 to 10Very highAvoid being outside around midday where you can. Shade, clothing and sunscreen are essential.
11 and aboveExtremeAs for very high. Unprotected skin can burn very quickly.

When you need sunscreen, use a broad-spectrum one with a sun protection factor (SPF) of at least 30, apply it generously and reapply it after swimming or sweating.

When UV is strongest

UV is strongest when the sun is highest in the sky, around the middle of the day. In many places that means roughly 11 am to 3 pm, a little later when clocks are on summer time. It's also stronger:

  • in summer than in winter,
  • closer to the equator,
  • at higher altitude, by roughly 10% for every 1,000 metres you climb.

Clouds and reflections

Thin or broken cloud lets much of the sun's UV through, so it's possible to burn on a cool, overcast day without noticing. Some surfaces also reflect UV back at you: fresh snow can reflect up to 80% of it, which is why skiers burn easily. Sand, water and sea foam reflect less, but still add to your exposure.

Reading UV on Hourly Forecast

The UV column in the hourly table shows the forecast index for each hour. It's zero at night and climbs through the morning to a peak around midday. If you'll be outside in the middle of a summer day, check the peak value and plan shade and sunscreen around it.
