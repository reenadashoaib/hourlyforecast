Humidity and dew point: why some days feel sticky

Updated 26 November 2026

Humidity is why a summer afternoon can feel oppressive at a temperature that would be pleasant in spring. Understanding how it's measured makes the forecast much easier to read.

Relative humidity

The humidity figure in most forecasts, including the one on this site, is relative humidity. It compares how much water vapour the air holds with the most it could hold at that temperature, as a percentage. At 100%, the air is saturated.

Warm air can hold much more water vapour than cold air. So even if the amount of moisture stays the same all day, relative humidity usually falls as the afternoon warms up and rises again overnight as the air cools. That's why humidity is often highest around dawn.

Why 90% isn't always muggy

Because relative humidity depends on temperature, the same percentage can feel very different. On a cold winter morning, 90% humidity feels damp and raw. On a hot summer afternoon, 90% feels stifling, because there's far more actual moisture in warm air and sweat can barely evaporate.

As a rough guide, high humidity starts to feel sticky once the temperature is above about 24°C (75°F).

Dew point: a better comfort guide

The dew point is the temperature the air would need to cool to for its moisture to condense. Unlike relative humidity, it measures the actual amount of moisture in the air, so it's a more direct guide to how muggy it feels. Many weather services use rules of thumb like these:

Dew pointHow it feels
Below 10°C (50°F)Dry and comfortable
10 to 16°C (50 to 60°F)Comfortable to slightly humid
16 to 18°C (60 to 65°F)Noticeably sticky
18 to 21°C (65 to 70°F)Muggy
Above 21°C (70°F)Oppressive

Hourly Forecast shows relative humidity rather than dew point. To judge mugginess, read the humidity together with the temperature for the same hour, or check the feels-like temperature, which already accounts for humidity.

Dew, frost and fog

On clear, calm nights the ground cools quickly. When it drops to the dew point, moisture condenses on grass and car windows as dew, or as frost if the temperature is below freezing. If the air just above the ground cools to its dew point, tiny water droplets form in the air itself, and you get fog.

A forecast showing humidity close to 100% overnight with light winds is a good hint that mist or fog may form by morning. When it's expected, the conditions column shows fog.

Humidity indoors

Outdoor humidity affects indoor comfort too. A common guideline for homes is to keep indoor relative humidity between about 40% and 60%. Much drier air can irritate eyes and skin; much damper air encourages condensation and mould.
