Feels-like temperature explained

Updated 26 November 2026

Two days can both read 5°C and feel completely different. On one it's still and sunny; on the other a cold wind cuts through your coat. The feels-like temperature tries to capture that difference.

Why the thermometer isn't the whole story

Your body is constantly trading heat with the air around it. How fast you lose or gain heat depends on more than the air temperature:

  • Wind carries away the thin layer of warm air next to your skin, so you cool faster.
  • Humidity slows the evaporation of sweat, so in hot weather you can't cool down as easily.
  • Sunshine warms you directly, which is why a sunny winter day can feel milder than the number suggests.

Wind chill in cold weather

When it's cold and windy, the feels-like temperature drops below the air temperature. For example, an air temperature of 0°C (32°F) with a wind of 30 km/h (19 mph) feels like about −6°C (21°F) on exposed skin. The stronger the wind, the bigger the gap.

Heat and humidity in warm weather

In hot, humid weather the feels-like temperature rises above the air temperature, because sweat can't evaporate quickly enough to cool you. An air temperature of 32°C (90°F) at 60% humidity feels closer to 38°C (100°F). That extra strain on the body is why humid heat waves are more dangerous than dry ones at the same temperature.

How Hourly Forecast works it out

The feels-like figures on this site are Open-Meteo's apparent temperature, which combines air temperature with wind, humidity and solar radiation. You'll find it under the current temperature at the top of the page, and in the "Feels like" column of the hourly table.

Dress for feels like, judge ice by the real temperature

Use the feels-like number to decide what to wear: it's a better guide to how cold your hands and face will get. But wind chill only affects things that are warmer than the air, like people and animals. It can't make water freeze. Puddles and roads only ice over when the actual air or ground temperature is at or below freezing, so check the real temperature for that.

Staying safe in extremes

Very low feels-like temperatures raise the risk of frostbite on exposed skin, and very high ones raise the risk of heat exhaustion. If the forecast shows extreme values, follow the advice of your national weather service and health service.
